How well do you really know your skin?

Most people know their height, their shoe size and their blood group — yet they are unaware of the three factors that visibly determine how their skin ages.

02How strong is your genetic protection against free radicals and UV exposure?Some people need more antioxidants than others — this predisposition is written in your genes.

Starting point

Why your skin is more than just a skin type.

The traditional classification of skin types — dry, oily, sensitive, mature — is an oversimplification that does not do justice to the reality of your skin. Your skin reacts to sleep, stress, diet, sun exposure and factors that are genetically determined.

Scientific reviews show that genetic factors account for up to 60 per cent of the variability in skin ageing. The remaining 40 per cent is attributable to lifestyle, the environment and epigenetic regulation — in other words, factors that can be consciously influenced. This is precisely where we come in.

Ready to truly understand your skin? Let’s start with a chat.

The mechanism behind it

Epigenetics meets cosmetics.

Your code is written — but not every chapter has been opened. What your genes do also depends on how you live and look after yourself.

Genetics

The personal blueprint.

The genome, stored in the DNA, contains all the instructions for proteins, hormones and enzymes. This blueprint remains unchanged throughout a person’s life.

vs
Epigenetics

The light switch.

Through mechanisms such as methylation, it controls which genes are active. A large proportion of these processes — around 70 per cent — can be influenced through diet, exercise, sleep, balance and targeted care.

You cannot rewrite your code — but you decide how it is presented.

What lies within your 20 beauty genes

The eight factors that influence the ageing of your skin.

Everyone ages differently — even with the same lifestyle. From 20 analysed gene variations, eight key factors can be identified that help shape how your skin changes. Once you know these, you no longer have to guess at your own skin’s needs; instead, you understand it — and care for it more consciously. It is precisely these 20 beauty genes that the Beauty Sensor reads — the skin analysis provided by your DNA Skin Concept.

01Collagen metabolismFine linesDeclining elasticityThinning skinFine lines, a loss of firmness and skin that appears thinner are linked to age-related changes in collagen metabolism — the balance between the synthesis and breakdown of the structural protein collagen. The efficiency with which the skin utilises protein building blocks such as proline for regeneration varies from person to person; a carefully tailored skincare routine can help maintain a well-cared-for, smooth appearance.
02Skin hydrationDry patchesFeeling of tensionScaly areasDry patches, a feeling of tightness and flaky areas may indicate that the skin is retaining moisture less effectively than it used to. Hyaluronic acid, the skin’s most important moisture reservoir, is broken down in the skin tissue by factors including UVB radiation — how well the skin compensates for this loss determines its suppleness. Moisturising skincare can help support this process.
03UV protectionPigment spotsUneven complexionLight-induced drynessUV radiation is one of the most severe external stresses on the skin and contributes to an uneven complexion and photoageing. The skin’s natural protective capacity varies from person to person; consistent use of sun protection helps it to maintain its well-cared-for appearance.
04Oxidative stressDull complexionUneven skin textureEarly signs of exhaustionA sallow complexion, an uneven skin texture and early signs of tiredness are often linked to oxidative stress — the strain placed on cells by free radicals from UV exposure, the environment and everyday stress. Some people neutralise these more effectively than others; antioxidant-based skincare can support the skin in everyday life.
05Coenzyme Q10Delayed regenerationLack of radianceProlonged wound healingCoenzyme Q10 is a substance produced naturally by the body that plays a central role in the energy metabolism of the mitochondria — the cell’s powerhouses — whilst also acting as an antioxidant. Over time, its levels in the skin decline; in cosmetics, Q10 is used to complement skincare with an antioxidant-based approach.
06Selenium requirementsWeak skin barrierIncreased sensitivityCommon reactionsSelenium is an essential trace element and a building block of the body’s own protective enzymes, such as glutathione peroxidase. According to scientifically proven data, selenium helps to protect cells from oxidative stress. The amount the body requires varies from person to person — the basis is a balanced intake through diet.
07Prone to inflammationRednessSkin imperfectionsSensitive phasesRedness, blemishes and periods of sensitivity may indicate that the skin is particularly reactive. The extent of this tendency varies from person to person; gentle, soothing skincare products can help to balance the skin’s condition and harmonise the skin’s natural environment.
08Biological skin ageingSkin appears older or youngerTelomere shorteningRate of cellular ageingBiological skin age describes how the skin appears in relation to its chronological age. It is linked to the length of telomeres — the protective caps at the ends of chromosomes, which shorten as cells divide. As an objective assessment, it provides valuable guidance for personalised skincare and is not a substitute for a medical evaluation.
